# Introduction

Since Stanley Cohen’s foundational work on moral panic, the concepts of *folk devils*, *moral entrepreneurs*, and *moral panic* have become central to the sociological study of deviance, social control, and collective reaction. Classical approaches conceptualized moral panic as a process in which a person, group, or social phenomenon is publicly constructed as a threat to dominant social values, provoking heightened concern, hostility, and demands for regulation or punishment (Goode & Ben-Yehuda 1994; Garland 2008). Within this framework, *folk devils* function as symbolically simplified embodiments of deviance, while *moral entrepreneurs* act as claims-makers who define, promote, and institutionalize moral boundaries through political, media, legal, or activist interventions.

Although the moral panic framework emerged from analyses of mass-mediated episodes of social reaction, contemporary scholarship increasingly examines how panic dynamics operate within fragmented and digitally networked communication environments. Researchers have explored the transformation of moral regulation under conditions of participatory media, algorithmic amplification, and decentralized communication flows (McRobbie & Thornton 1995; Falkof 2020; Carlson 2020). Recent studies of so-called *networked moral panics* argue that panic processes are no longer driven exclusively by centralized mass media institutions, but increasingly emerge through recursive interactions between online actors, platform infrastructures, and distributed publics (Obrien 2023; Lemish 2023).

At the same time, the conceptual expansion of moral panic research has generated important theoretical and methodological tensions. The framework has been applied to an increasingly diverse range of phenomena, including cybercrime (Lavorgna 2019), misinformation (Moran 2023), migration (Zhang 2025), educational conflicts (Leeds 2024), gender politics (Shiran 2024), and populist mobilization (Amery 2025). While this demonstrates the continued relevance of the concept, critics argue that moral panic research risks becoming analytically diffuse and conceptually overstretched (Critcher 2008; Cohen 2011; Hier 2023). In particular, debates persist regarding disproportionality, temporality, agency, and the empirical identification of panic processes in contemporary media environments (Rohloff 2010; Hier 2024).

One important limitation of the existing literature is the relative fragmentation between the core elements of the moral panic framework itself. Studies frequently examine *moral entrepreneurs*, *folk devils*, and *moral panic* as analytically distinct phenomena rather than as dynamically interacting processes. Research on moral entrepreneurs often focuses on norm advocacy, political mobilization, or institutional claims-making (Flores-Yeffal 2020; Buntinx 2021), while studies of folk devils primarily investigate symbolic stigmatization and media representation (Mikhaylova 2022). Moral panic research, meanwhile, tends to emphasize macro-level discourse, media amplification, or public reaction. As a result, the interactive relationship between strategic moral claims-making, stigmatization, peer conformity, and network structure remains insufficiently formalized.

This limitation becomes especially significant in digitally networked environments, where panic escalation increasingly depends on decentralized interaction rather than solely on top-down media transmission. Existing accounts of networked moral panic acknowledge distributed communication dynamics, yet many continue to treat moral entrepreneurs and folk devils as relatively fixed and stable social categories. Such approaches underestimate the strategic and adaptive agency of actors embedded in networked publics. In practice, actors may contest stigmatizing labels, strategically amplify outrage, exploit moralized narratives, or shift between positions of moral authority and deviant attribution depending on the communicative context. Consequently, panic escalation should be understood not as a linear media effect, but as a contingent and interactive process shaped by strategic intervention, social reinforcement, and network topology.

A related challenge concerns methodology. Existing studies of moral panic rely predominantly on discourse analysis, media framing analysis, qualitative case studies, or descriptive examinations of online communication. While these approaches provide substantial interpretive insight, they offer limited capacity to examine how micro-level interaction produces macro-level escalation dynamics over time. More specifically, the literature still lacks formalized tools capable of systematically examining how targeted moral intervention, peer conformity, and heterogeneous network structures interact to generate polarization, amplification, persistence, or containment of moralized social reactions (Mikhaylova 2021; Mikhaylova 2022).

This article addresses this gap by proposing an agent-based model of moral influence and social conformity designed to examine the networked micro-dynamics underlying moral panic escalation. Rather than attempting to simulate moral panic as a fully reducible social phenomenon, the study focuses on the structural conditions under which panic escalation becomes more or less likely within networked social systems. In this context, panic escalation refers to the amplification, persistence, polarization, and clustering of moralized social reactions generated through the interaction of strategic intervention, peer conformity, and network topology.

The model operationalizes two competing external claims-makers who strategically target agents embedded within different network structures while agents simultaneously respond to local peer influence. Each agent carries two coupled states — a moral alignment and a level of perceived threat — and updates them through the interaction of several mechanisms: targeted external intervention, conformity to ideologically proximate neighbors combined with repulsion from distant ones, threat contagion and threshold-driven activation, the conversion of local disagreement into perceived threat ("othering"), and the persistence of prior convictions. This design foregrounds perceived threat — the exaggerated danger that claims-makers construct and amplify — as the engine of escalation, and lets polarization, clustering, and the stigmatized "folk devil" position emerge from interaction rather than being imposed. The study therefore examines how different targeting strategies, network topologies, and levels of conformity influence the emergence of polarization, clustering, escalation, and stabilization dynamics within moralized communication environments.

Conceptually, the article builds on scholarship in moral panic theory, norm entrepreneurship, and computational social science (Finnemore & Sikkink 1998; Hollander 2010). However, unlike classical norm diffusion models that primarily examine institutional norm cascades, the present study focuses on the decentralized and contested dynamics of moral amplification within heterogeneous social networks. The article therefore asks the following research question: under what network conditions do competing moral claims-makers generate escalation, polarization, or containment of moralized social reactions through the interaction of targeted influence, peer conformity and repulsion, and threat amplification?

The article contributes to the literature in three ways. First, it formalizes the interaction between moral entrepreneurs, folk devils, and moral panic processes within a dynamic interactional framework. Second, it introduces a formalized computational approach for examining the interaction between strategic agency, peer conformity, and network topology. Third, it contributes to the emerging literature on networked moral panics by shifting analytical attention from static representations and episodic discourse toward emergent interactional processes and escalation dynamics.

Rather than conceptualizing moral panic as a singular or uniform outcome, the article argues that panic escalation should be understood as a contingent process emerging from the interaction between strategic moral intervention, social conformity, and networked communication structures.
